## A. Why this test does not claim clinical competence

The content used is **PRC-source interpretation**, not a medical diagnosis or intervention: a fictional independent reviewer editor deciding what can be claimed from (1) a dated official examination program and (2) a scanned TOS with documented missing detailed rows. This is an educational source-literacy case. It is **not** a board question and does not establish how many situational items the PRC actually asks.

**Verified project premise:** the source report records five official examination subject headings and notes that the scanned 2013 TOS's Primary Health Care detailed list begins at A.12, leaving A.1–A.11 unrecoverable from that copy. [Research report](Komadrona-Online-Evidence-Report.md). A PRC examination program does not supply these missing detailed TOS lines. OD-001 requires honest distinction between subject presence and unverified detailed coverage.

## B. Three contrasting instructional treatments of the *same source concept*

**A — Direct note + immediate MCQ**
- Present one paragraph describing the five subject headings and missing detailed rows.
- Immediately show the source-interpretation MCQ, then show only the correct option.
- No worked source-comparison, no rationale for distractors, no later revisit.
- Benefit hypothesis: short and fast. Risk hypothesis: recognition/guessing and shallow source interpretation.

**B — Guided source comparison + effortful recall + fictional situation + delayed review**
- Learner sees PRC program vs TOS **as different document purposes**; contrasts what each can legitimately support.
- Worked nonclinical example: an editor may cite the program for subject list but must identify unknown detail when the TOS scan omits it.
- Learner answers, before reveal: “Can the exam schedule itself recover a missing TOS competency?”
- Then answers the fictional scenario MCQ and sees the supported key, distractor explanations, dated source/provenance and option to revisit a semantically related, **nonidentical** future item.
- Benefit hypothesis: richer explanation and transfer. Risk hypothesis: time/overload; must remain accessible in plain text.

**C — Question first + hint on error**
- Learner receives the same source-interpretation situation before explanation.
- On first error, offer a hint distinguishing exam program vs TOS and a second attempt.
- Key rationale shown at end, but no full worked comparison or later schedule.
- Benefit hypothesis: efficient for prepared repeaters. Risk hypothesis: confusing for novice and inflated score from hint/exposure.

### Single original, nonclinical scenario example — PAPER ONLY

**Context:** A fictional editor is building a study page. The official examination program visibly lists **Primary Health Care** as a subject. The available scanned detailed TOS for that subject begins at competency A.12; A.1–A.11 are not visible in that scan. The editor wants to write that the page has exact official competency coverage.

**Prompt:** Which publication decision can the editor defend from those two sources?

1. Treat A.1–A.11 as fully verified because Primary Health Care appears in the official exam program.
2. Keep Primary Health Care in the navigation and clearly mark the missing detailed competencies **unverified** until exact source evidence is found.
3. Remove Primary Health Care from the subject list until every detailed competency can be transcribed.
4. Replace the missing TOS wording with a general curriculum topic list and label it **verbatim PRC TOS**.

**Defensible choice for this source-literacy exercise:** **2**, because the subject heading is supported, but missing detailed TOS text is not. Option 1 falsely infers detail from broad subject presence; option 3 confuses lack of detail with absence of an official subject; option 4 misattributes curriculum or inferred wording as official. This rationale is grounded in the **source report and OD-001** and is not a medical claim.

**Important editorial caveat:** The option wording contains telltale phrases (“fully verified”, “verbatim”, “clearly mark unverified”); they may cue the correct answer without reasoning. A true item-writing reviewer must refine this if ever used as a scored item. It has **not** been independently validated, student-tested, or released.

## D. Genuine discovered defects, not fabricated victories

1. **DEF-001 (HIGH design):** Common MCQ wording strongly signals the "careful" answer. This undermines HYP-004 assessment validity and makes immediate item correctness weak evidence of transfer. **Repair experiment:** rewrite options to reduce cue imbalance, then get independent item review. Do not compare knowledge gains using a flawed item.
2. **DEF-002 (HIGH design):** Even preferred variant B lacks a concrete revocation-aware revisit specification. **Repair:** connect due-review eligibility to exact current item/source and revocation decision, then replay withdrawal/rollback scenario on paper.
3. **DEF-003 (MEDIUM):** A and C do not specify a learner-facing disclaimer about what mock accuracy means. **Repair:** standardize honest score semantics across modes.
4. **DEF-004 (UNCERTAIN):** B's added steps might create unnecessary time or reading load for experienced learners. **Repair:** contrast a novice path with a direct review path and plan equal-time controlled comparisons.
5. **DEF-005 (UNTESTED):** No screen-reader, phone, or multilingual comprehension study was executed. Text-first is not automatically WCAG compliance.
